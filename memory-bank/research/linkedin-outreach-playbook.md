# LinkedIn Outreach Playbook

## Golden Rule
The connection request is NOT the pitch. It's the door. Walk through it empty-handed. Introduce the tool in the REPLY after they connect.

## What Works
- Ask a real question about THEIR process
- Reference something specific you noticed (community name, design center photo, recent award, floor plan)
- Make it about their world, not your product
- End on the question — let the question BE the close

## What Doesn't Work
- Leading with the product description
- Copy-pasting the same core sentence with name/company swaps
- "Would love to get your take" as a verbal tic on every message
- Generic qualifiers like "with your sales background" that apply to anyone
- Inventing shared connections or details (DON'T say you're local, went to their school, etc. unless it's TRUE)

## The Template Trap
If you can swap company names and the message still works with zero other changes, it's a template. Builders have been pitched their whole careers — they can feel the difference. Five well-researched messages that start real conversations > twenty templates that get polite ignores.

## Five Angles to Rotate

### 1. The Question (no pitch)
Ask about their current process. No mention of the tool. Best for design center managers and online sales roles.
> "Hi [Name], how does [Company] handle design selections right now? Sample boards and in-person appointments, or do you have anything online for buyers?"

### 2. The Observation
Notice something specific about their company. Tie the outreach to that observation.
> "Hi [Name], [Company]'s design studio looks great. Curious how buyers share their selections with family after the appointment, or preview options before they come in."

### 3. The Tinkerer Intro
"I've been building something..." but make the WHY specific to their world. Best for CEOs/presidents.
> "Hi [Name], your homes in [location] are well done. I've been building a tool that shows buyers what their upgrade picks look like together in actual room photos — instead of imagining it from individual samples. Would love to get your take if you're open."

### 4. The Shared Context
Reference a market condition, trend, or something from a builder publication. Best for VPs and division-level.
> "Hi [Name], I saw that [specific thing] and it made me think about [related problem]. Curious how [Company] approaches that."

### 5. The Proof Point (use later, once you have traction)
Reference conversations with other builders.
> "Hi [Name], I showed this to a builder in [market] and they said X. I'm curious if you'd see it differently."

## Closes (rotate these too)
- "Happy to show you what it looks like if you're curious."
- "No pitch — just like talking to people who actually run this process."
- "Would be great to hear how [Company] thinks about this."
- Or just end on the question. No additional ask needed.

## Pre-Message Checklist (90 seconds)
Before writing each message, find ONE specific thing:
- [ ] A community name from their website
- [ ] A design center photo from their Instagram
- [ ] A floor plan name
- [ ] A quote from a press release
- [ ] A recent LinkedIn post or award
- [ ] Their Zillow listing showing standard vs. upgrade finishes

That one detail is the difference between "this person looked at my company" and "this is a LinkedIn pitch."

## Who to Target (priority order)
1. **VP/Director of Sales & Marketing** — owns the upgrade revenue number, can champion and buy
2. **VP of Design / Design Studio Director** — owns the selection experience at a senior level
3. **President/CEO** — decision-maker, but only if the company is small enough that they're hands-on
4. **Region/Division President** — owns P&L for their market, cares about revenue per home

## Who to Skip
- Purchasing Managers (supply side, not buyer side)
- VP of Operations / Construction (build side, not sales side)
- CFO/Controller (finance brain, not experience brain)
- Community Sales Managers / New Home Sales Counselors (too junior, no authority to buy)
- Online Sales Consultants / Coordinators (feel the gap but can't act on it)
- Marketing Content Strategists / Brand Storytellers (describe the problem accurately but don't own the number)
- Design Studio Managers (unless at a very small builder where they have real influence — at larger builders they don't have budget authority)
- Anyone with < 50 LinkedIn connections or zero activity

## Deprioritize
- Builders already on Envision Options or Roomored — they feel "solved" even though they're not. Come back with case studies.
- Builders acquired by nationals (D.R. Horton, Clayton/Berkshire) — corporate procurement is slow.

## The Research → Message Process (Step by Step)

This is how we actually craft each message. Takes 5-10 minutes per builder. The research IS the message — you're not writing copy, you're finding the one thing that makes it real.

### Step 1: Pick the Right Person (2 min)
Go to the builder's LinkedIn company page → People tab. Look for:
- VP of Sales & Marketing (ideal — owns the number AND the experience)
- Design Studio/Center Manager (feels the daily pain)
- President/CEO (only if small builder where they're hands-on)

**Red flags to skip someone:**
- Their primary role is at a different company (contractor/agency, not full-time)
- Zero posts and < 100 connections (not active on LinkedIn)
- Title is operations, construction, purchasing, or finance
- Too junior to champion anything internally

If there's a new VP (< 1 year in role), prioritize them — new leaders want quick wins.

### Step 2: Research the Builder (3-5 min)
Check these sources for ONE specific detail:
1. **Their LinkedIn activity** — recent posts, shared articles, awards, hiring
2. **Their website** — community names, design center photos, buyer journey page, included features
3. **Google "[Builder Name] design center"** or "[Builder Name] design studio" — see what comes up
4. **The contact's personal LinkedIn** — career history, past roles, anything that reveals what they care about
5. **Awards/press** — OBIE, Nationals, HBA, ProBuilder rankings

You're looking for ONE of these:
- A specific community name + what makes it notable
- A design center/studio detail (or the absence of one)
- A recent post they made about a specific topic
- Something from their career history that connects to design/visualization
- A campaign, award, or initiative they're proud of

### Step 3: Write the Message (2 min)
Formula: **[Specific observation] → [Genuine question about their process]**

That's it. The specific observation proves you looked. The question invites a response. No product, no pitch, no ask beyond the question itself.

**Test it:** Read the message back. If you could send it to a different builder by changing the company name, it's a template. Rewrite it.

### Step 4: After They Accept (the follow-up sequence)

#### The Loom Play (primary follow-up for high-value prospects)

Text from a stranger is easy to ignore. A 60-second video using their actual community photos is not. This is the single highest-leverage thing you can do after a connection accept.

**The process (10-15 min per prospect):**

1. **Grab a room photo** from their community. Sources:
   - Their website (model home gallery, virtual tour screenshots)
   - Zillow/Realtor.com listings for their communities (interior photos)
   - Their Instagram or Facebook (model home walkthroughs)
   - Google Images "[Builder] [Community Name] interior"

2. **Run it through Finch.** Use the Demo org or set up a quick version. Pick 3-4 upgrades that are visually obvious: countertops, cabinets, flooring, backsplash. Generate the before and after.

3. **Record a 60-90 second Loom.** Structure:
   - "Hey [Name], I grabbed this photo from your [Community/Model] and wanted to show you something."
   - Show the base room photo. "This is what your buyers see today."
   - Click through 2-3 upgrade selections. Show the generated result. "And this is what it looks like when they pick [quartz counters / shaker cabinets / herringbone tile]."
   - "Imagine your buyers seeing this before they commit. That's what I've been building. Thought you'd want to see it with your actual homes. Happy to talk more if you're curious."

4. **Send the Loom link** as the follow-up message on LinkedIn. Keep the text short. The video does the work.

**The message:**

> "Hey [Name], I grabbed a photo from your [Community] model and ran it through what I've been building. Easier to show than explain: [Loom link]"

That's it. No pitch paragraph. No feature list. The video IS the pitch.

**Why this works:**
- Can't be templated. It uses THEIR photos. They know you put in the work.
- Shows the product without asking them to take a meeting first.
- Video gets 3-5x the engagement of text on LinkedIn.
- It's the "problem proposition" but shown, not told. They see the before (PDF/swatch world) and the after (their room with real selections) in 60 seconds.
- Even if they don't reply, they watched it. You're no longer a stranger next time.

**When to use it:**
- High-value prospects (CEOs, VPs, design center managers at target builders)
- Anyone who accepted the connection but didn't reply to a text follow-up
- Builders where you can easily find interior model photos online

**When to skip it:**
- Can't find a usable room photo (don't force it with a stock photo, that defeats the purpose)
- Very small builders where the time investment doesn't justify the deal size
- They already replied to your text. Just have the conversation.

**Loom tips:**
- Don't over-produce it. Screen share + your face in the corner is fine. Casual > polished.
- Keep it under 90 seconds. They'll watch 60 seconds from a stranger. They won't watch 3 minutes.
- Say their name and their community name. It's a pattern interrupt that signals "this is for me, not a blast."
- End with curiosity, not a hard ask. "Thought you'd want to see it" not "Let's book a call."

The connection request earned you the door. Now you need to earn the conversation. Same principle as the cold call opener: context first, problem before product, small yes before big ask.

**Don't immediately pitch.** What you do depends on what happened:

#### Path A: They accepted AND replied to your question
Have the conversation. Stay in their world. When they tell you how their process works, that's your opening to introduce the problem naturally.

If they describe sample boards, binders, PDF option sheets, or in-person-only selections, that's the triggering problem. You don't need to tell them it's a problem. Just connect what they said to what you built:

> "That's actually what got me started on this. I kept hearing the same thing from builders. Buyers are picking from samples or a sheet, they can't picture how it all comes together in the room, so they play it safe and stick closer to the base package. I built something that lets them see their actual selections in the room photos before they commit. Started with a builder in Alabama and it's been interesting to watch buyers spend more when they can picture what they're getting."

Then the interest check (not the meeting ask yet):

> "Does that sound like something that'd change anything for your buyers, or is your process already working well enough?"

If they say yes or express curiosity, then ask for the meeting:

> "Happy to show you what it looks like. Takes about 15 minutes and I can pull up real options. Would that be worth it?"

#### Path B: They accepted but DIDN'T reply
Send a short follow-up that stays in their world. Reference something new and specific (a recent post, a community update). No product yet. You're building familiarity.

If they still don't reply after the second message, you can introduce the problem. Keep it short. Lead with the pain, not the product:

> "Hey [Name], one more thing and I'll stop bugging you. I've been talking to builders about the selections process and the same thing keeps coming up. Buyers pick upgrades off a sheet, they can't picture what it looks like together, so they play it safe. I built something that shows them their picks in the actual room. Happy to show you a quick example if you're curious. If not, no worries at all."

#### Path C: They replied with useful info about their process
This is the best case. Ground the tool introduction in what they just told you. Reference their specific words.

> "[Something they said] is exactly what I keep hearing. That's actually why I started building this..."

Then follow the same sequence: problem, one-sentence solution, interest check, meeting ask.

### Multiple-Choice Questions (keep conversations going)

Borrowed from the cold call framework. When someone gives you a short reply or you need to keep the conversation moving, give them 2-3 options to pick from. It makes answering easy and shows you know the space.

**When they describe their process vaguely:**
> "Is it mostly handled in person at the design center, or do buyers get anything to look at online before the appointment?"

**When they say they have something already:**
> "Is it working well for you, or is it more that switching would be a hassle?"

**When they seem interested but noncommittal:**
> "Would it be more useful for me to send you a link to click through yourself, or would 15 minutes on a call be better so I can show you with your options?"

Don't stack multiple questions. One at a time. The question should feel like you're genuinely curious which bucket they fall in, not steering them toward an answer.

### The Follow-Up Message (when you introduce the tool)

Rules:
- Lead with the problem, not the product. Paint the scene before you offer the fix.
- Ground it in a real builder relationship ("started with a builder in Alabama")
- Interest check before meeting ask. "Does that match what you're seeing?" comes before "Want me to show you?"
- End on a question, not a statement
- Never say "AI." The tech is invisible.

### Examples: What Good Looks Like

**Bad (template):**
> "Hi [Name], I've built a tool that helps builders visualize upgrade selections. Would love to show you how it works."

**Good (researched connection request):**
> "Hi Mary, I actually came across your work with the design studio at Viera. Winning the Nationals Silver for that is no small thing. Curious how the selection experience works at McKinley now that you're scaling across five states. Are buyers getting any kind of preview before the design appointment, or is it still mostly in-person?"

**Good (follow-up after they describe their process):**
> "That makes sense. The thing I keep hearing from builders is that the in-person appointment works fine, but buyers show up not knowing what anything looks like together. They pick a countertop and a cabinet and flooring, and they're hoping it all works. I built something where they see those selections in the actual room photos before they commit. Started with a builder in Montgomery and it's been interesting to see how much more buyers spend when they can picture it. Worth a quick look?"

The difference: the bad one is about you. The good ones are about them, grounded in something specific, and ask a question they'd actually want to answer.
